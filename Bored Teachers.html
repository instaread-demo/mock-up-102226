13 Ridiculous Reasons Teachers Got Written Up by Admin

by Angela Barton

Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 5:06
Instaread

In any profession, employees can be written up for not meeting expectations or doing their job effectively.

But teaching is a little different. Sometimes teachers get write-ups so unrelated to teaching and learning that you can only sit there, stare at the paperwork, and question if you are actually living in a Twilight Zone episode.

These aren’t stories about serious misconduct or teachers failing their students. These are the bizarre, petty, and downright unbelievable reasons teachers have gotten into trouble with administration.

Here are 13 of the most ridiculous reasons teachers have been written up by admin. And yes, these actually happened.

1. Wearing Denim

It seems a pair of jeans can somehow interfere with a teacher’s ability to educate the next generation. Forget content, classroom management, and student motivation…the real problem was dark blue denim.

Because apparently, teachers can handle 30 energetic children, endless paperwork, and no help with disruptive students, but wearing comfortable pants is where we must draw the line.

2. Leaving Early

Imagine working in a profession where staying late, answering emails after hours, grading papers at home, and coming in early are simply considered part of the job, but leaving a few minutes early somehow becomes “theft of services.”

One teacher left work early to pick up her own children, and what could have been a simple conversation about the circumstances turned into a formal disciplinary issue.

Of course, all those unpaid minutes we give away don’t count, but take a few back, and suddenly we’re criminals. Lock us up; we will probably have more freedom in the slammer.

3. Showing a Movie the Day Before Any Break

The day before a break is the perfect time to give students a fun memory before they head home for the holiday. One teacher showed a movie to help make the day special and keep students engaged.

Instead of appreciating the effort to create a positive classroom experience, admin turned it into a write-up. Shameful.

4. Looking at One Side of the Room More Than the Other

Apparently, even your eye contact needs to be equitable.

One teacher was criticized for looking at one side of the classroom more than the other.

Imagine being in an evaluation meeting and hearing that your biggest problem was essentially, “You looked over there too much.”

5. Taking Two Days to Grade a Quiz

Two days.

Two days. That’s how long one teacher took to grade a quiz, and admin decided that was too long, resulting in a write-up for not providing “timely feedback.”

Yet, it takes five business days for administration to respond to an email.

6. Too Loud in the Hallway

We can pull out every trick in the classroom-management book…quiet signals, reminders, proximity, positive reinforcement, you name it.

Sometimes, kids will just be kids and just talk.

7. Leaving the Classroom to Throw Up While Pregnant

One pregnant teacher had to step into the lab prep room to throw up and got written up for leaving her students unsupervised.

Clearly, the expectation is that teachers should either throw up in front of 25 kids or hold it until recess.

8. A Student Singing

One student starts singing Acapella during a lockdown drill, and the teacher gets blamed, of course.

Because……. teachers are responsible for everything, including surprise concerts during emergency drills.

9. Too Many Chairs

A teacher was questioned about extra chairs—even though they were there for a practical reason: backup seating when classes had to be split because of substitute shortages.

The teacher was planning ahead.

Somehow, even chairs need an explanation.

10. The Projector Breaking

The projector failed during a lesson, and the teacher was written up for not testing it beforehand.

At this point, teachers don’t need an IT department. They are the IT department.

11. Standards (God forbid) weren’t written on the board

The lesson was aligned to the standards.

The students were learning.

The teacher was teaching.

But the standards weren’t written on the board.

Because apparently, the lesson can be perfect, but if the standard isn’t on the wall, it’s pure crap.

12. A Coffee cup with no lid

A teacher was written up for walking down the hallway with a coffee cup without a lid.

Not drinking during class.
Not spilling coffee on students.
Just walking with an uncovered cup.

Teachers, please cover your mugs, STAT!

13. The Classroom Was Too Quiet.

A teacher was questioned because the classroom was too quiet during independent work.

Students were focused. They were working. No one was disrupting anyone.

Too noisy? Problem.

Too quiet? Also a problem. 

Is this classroom management or Goldilocks?

The Bottom Line

Teachers understand that accountability is part of the job. Yeah, we get that loud and clear. But not every awkward moment, technical failure, or unpredictable student behavior needs to become a formal write-up.

Kids are unpredictable. Technology fails. Things happen.

Teachers already spend every day solving problems and going the extra mile without extra pay.

Maybe we don’t need another write-up.

Maybe we just need a little common sense, a little grace, and occasionally, a sense of humor.

Humor teacher life

A Professor Set a Genius AI Trap for His Students. 32 Out of 35 Fell for It.

by Angela Barton

AI was supposed to make teaching easier, but instead teachers are spending more time policing student work, detecting AI-generated essays, and figuring out how to keep students learning. Remember when everyone said artificial intelligence was going to make teachers’ lives easier? And to be fair, it does….. somewhat. AI can crank out lesson plans, generate activities, differentiate assignments, and provide instant feedback in a matter of seconds.

But there’s one little problem: How do we get students to actually learn when AI can do so much of the work for them?

Suddenly, we aren’t just teaching and grading. We’re becoming AI inspectors, trying to determine whether the essay sitting in front of us was actually written by our student or a chatbot. We’re comparing writing samples, questioning vocabulary, and wondering how the kid who normally writes, “The Industrial Revolution was good,” suddenly sounds Harvard-educated.

Well, one professor had enough and came up with a great idea to prove AI was being used for more than just ideas.

The Professor Who Set a Trap for AI

A history professor at Alcorn State University recently went viral after revealing a rather clever way he caught students using AI on a midterm.

He inserted hidden text into the assignment using white font on a white background. Students couldn’t see it when they read the assignment normally, but when they copied and pasted the entire prompt into an AI chatbot, the hidden instruction went along with it.

The instruction told the AI to insert a completely random word into the response: “Madagascar.”

The assignment was about the Industrial Revolution. According to the professor, 32 of the 35 students submitted responses containing the bizarre hidden instruction. He said the students apparently didn’t even proofread the AI-generated responses before turning them in. That’s honestly not surprising.

Now We’re Analyzing Essays for AI

One of the biggest changes AI has brought to teaching is that teachers can no longer simply read an essay and focus on the student’s ideas. We’re now reading it with a completely different question in the back of our minds: Did the student actually write this?

We’re looking for writing that sounds “too good” for our students’ abilities. This becomes very difficult because it is so subjective. The frustrating part is that we want to trust our students instead of accusing someone of cheating.

We should be spending our time giving students feedback on their ideas, helping them become better writers, and encouraging them to develop their own voice. Instead, we’re analyzing word choice and judging writing ability. Teachers aren’t just grading essays anymore. We’re investigating them.

And Then We Were Told to Use AI Detectors

The solution to this problem, we were told, was supposed to be AI detectors.

Just run the essay through a program and let it tell us whether the student used AI. Sounds easy, right? Except it isn’t, and it isn’t accurate. AI detectors aren’t reliable enough to give teachers a clear yes-or-no answer. They can flag writing that was actually written by a student, while AI-generated writing can sometimes slip right through. A teacher can end up accusing a student of cheating based on a percentage that isn’t proof of anything.

So now we’re stuck in an impossible position. We’re expected to catch students who use AI, but the tools designed to help us catch them aren’t always accurate. We can’t simply look at a detector score and say, “Yep, got em.” That means we’re back to analyzing the essay ourselves, comparing it to previous work, questioning the student, and coming up with schemes like the “Madagascar Professor.”

So much for saving teachers time.

AI gave us a tool to save time, then created a problem that takes even more of it away from us. And somehow, the teacher is still the one responsible for figuring it all out. Of course we are. We always are.

So What Is the Solution?

Where does this leave teachers? One possible solution is taking more of the writing process back into the classroom. Instead of sending students home with an essay and hoping they actually wrote it, teachers can have students brainstorm, write, and revise during class. That makes it much easier to see their thinking and their writing process firsthand. Limit Chromebooks. Bring paper-and-pencil work back as the norm. Of course, this takes lots and lots of classroom time, but it ensures students are actually doing the work.

Maybe the answer isn’t to ban AI or spend our days trying to catch students using it. Maybe we need to teach students how to use AI responsibly while still making sure they are doing the thinking themselves. That’s the tough part. Human behavior wants to save time, but we don’t want to eliminate thinking altogether. AI can be an incredible tool for teachers and students, but it shouldn’t replace learning.

Because at the end of the day, that’s what this is really about. We’re not worried that students are using AI. We’re worried that they’re using it instead of learning.
