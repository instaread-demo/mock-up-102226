7 Checks Before Setting Up a Tablet Station in an Equipment Repair Shop

Your techs already pull up service manuals on their phones, squint at wiring diagrams and shout part numbers across the shop to whoever’s at the office computer. A shared tablet station on the shop floor fixes a lot of that. It puts machine records, manuals and parts lookups where the work actually happens.

It can also turn into an expensive screen nobody uses. A tablet that’s in the wrong spot, locked behind the wrong login or dead by 10 a.m. gets ignored fast. These seven checks will help you set one up that your team reaches for without thinking.

Check #1 – Decide What the Station Is For

Before you buy anything, write down the three or four jobs the station needs to do. In most repair shops, that list looks something like this:

  • Looking up a machine’s make, model and serial number from a work order
  • Opening the service manual or parts diagram for that machine
  • Identifying and checking replacement part numbers
  • Logging the work done and the parts used

Anything beyond that list is a bonus. Keeping the purpose narrow makes every later decision easier, including which apps go on the tablet and who can use it. A station that tries to be a parts counter, a time clock and a training screen all at once usually ends up doing none of them well.

Pick one owner for the station, too. That person keeps the apps updated, reports damage and decides when something on the home screen goes.

Check #2 – Confirm the Tablet and Apps Work Together

Shops often grab whatever tablet is lying around. That’s fine, as long as it runs everything the station needs. Spend an hour on this before you mount anything.

Start with the software. Some shop management systems and manufacturer service portals only run well on certain operating systems, or work in a browser but not as an app. Log in to each one on the tablet you plan to use, open a real work order and pull up a real diagram.

Screen Size and Readability

Parts diagrams and hydraulic schematics are dense. A small screen means constant pinching and zooming, often with gloved or greasy fingers. A 10-inch screen is a sensible minimum for most diagram work, and bigger is easier to read from a step back.

Gloves and Glare

Check the screen under your shop’s actual lighting, including any skylights or open bay doors. Then try it with the gloves your techs actually wear. If touch doesn’t register, a capacitive stylus on a tether is a cheap fix.

Check #3 – Make Sure the Mount Won’t Tip or Wobble

A tablet propped on a workbench will get knocked off or buried under parts. A shared station needs a fixed home that stays upright when a tech leans on it after a long day.

Before you settle on a spot, walk the routes that carts and machines take through the shop, and keep the station well clear of them. Then choose a mount built for a busy floor, such as a tablet floor stand from VidaBox with a weighted base, which keeps the screen at standing height without eating bench space. Once it’s placed, give it a firm push from a few directions before you trust it with a device.

Think about height and angle as well. Techs should be able to read the screen without bending, and the stand shouldn’t block sightlines across the shop floor.

Check #4 – Plan Power Before You Pick the Spot

A tablet that runs flat by mid-morning is worse than no tablet, because people stop trusting it. Plan the power route at the same time as the location.

The easiest setup is a wall outlet close enough that the charging cable runs a short, protected distance to the stand. Avoid a long extension cord across the floor. Under OSHA rules, flexible cords are prohibited as a substitute for fixed wiring unless specifically permitted, so if the best spot has no outlet nearby, talk to an electrician about adding one.

Use a charging cable rated for continuous use, and keep a spare in the office. Cables at shared stations wear out far sooner than the tablets do.

Check #5 – Test Wi-Fi Where the Station Will Stand

Office Wi-Fi often stops at the shop door. Steel racking, machines and block walls can weaken a signal that looks fine at the front desk.

Test from the exact spot with the tablet and try the real tasks: open a large parts diagram, load a manual, save a work order. If anything stalls, fix the network before you mount the station. A mesh access point or a wired access point in the shop usually costs less than the time techs lose waiting.

Find out what happens when the connection drops, too. Some shop apps save work offline and sync later, while others lose whatever was typed. Your techs should know which kind they’re using.

Check #6 – Set Up Access Controls That Fit a Shared Device

A shared tablet is still a door into your business systems. Customer records, pricing and supplier accounts may all be one tap away, so decide who can see what.

Give each tech their own login to the shop software instead of one shared account. That way the work log shows who did what, and you can remove access when someone leaves. For anything sensitive, such as ordering or customer billing, CISA recommends that businesses require multifactor authentication, which adds a second way to verify a user’s identity beyond the password.

A few device settings close the most obvious gaps:

  • Lock the screen after a short idle time
  • Turn off app installs for everyone except the station’s owner
  • Sign out of the shop software automatically at the end of each shift

Keep personal accounts off the device entirely, including email and messaging.

Check #7 – Map the Repair Information Workflow

This is where the station earns its spot on the floor. Walk through a typical job and note every point where the tablet gets used.

A good flow starts with the work order. The tech opens the machine’s record and confirms the make, model and serial number against the plate on the machine itself, since serial ranges often decide which parts fit. From there, the service manual and parts diagram should be two taps away.

Once a tech has identified the worn component, such as a cylinder seal on a compact excavator, the next step is matching the replacement to that exact machine. The tech can then search a catalog of mini excavator parts from HW Part Store by make and model, rather than relying on memory or a similar machine’s part list. Confirm the part number against the service manual before the order goes in.

Finish by logging the part and the date in the machine’s history. Record the serial number with every part order so the next tech working on that machine starts with the right information.

Start With a Single Bay

You don’t need a station in every bay on day one. Pick the bay that handles the most varied work, run through these seven checks there and let your techs use it for a few weeks. What they tell you will shape the next station better than any spec sheet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where should a tablet station go in an equipment repair shop?

Put it near the work, at standing height, clear of the routes carts and machines take through the shop. It also needs a nearby wall outlet and a Wi-Fi signal that handles large diagrams at that exact spot.

Should techs share one login on a shop tablet?

Give each tech their own login so the work log shows who did what and access can be removed when someone leaves. For ordering or billing, CISA recommends that businesses require multifactor authentication.

How do techs avoid ordering the wrong replacement part?

Confirm the make, model and serial number against the machine’s plate, then search parts by make and model. Check the part number against the service manual before the order goes in.
