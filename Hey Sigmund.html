Anxiety in Children & Teens

by Karen Young (BSc)(Psych)(Hons)MastGestTher

The Art of Warm and Firm: How to hold the boundary without losing the relationship.

Parenting has a particular way of bringing all of us to the same place eventually.

It usually starts with a perfectly reasonable no. Sometimes to a perfectly unreasonable request. Or an untimely one. 

Then, from them: the words, the spicy, the pleading, the fury, the accusations of ruined lives, and you don’t even care about me! 

And just like that, the confidence in us that felt so big and certain moments ago, can now fit through the eye of a needle, no trouble at all.

Ugh.

Then comes the question, often arriving with unapologetic gusto and urgency, ‘What do I do now?’

Do I get firmer? Softer?  Do I hold the boundary – or protect the relationship? (Can we just have one easy morning!?)

If only it were as simple as choosing one. But no. This is the exquisite difficulty of parenting: in the moments that stretch us most, our children need us to hold two things that can, at times, feel impossible to hold together.

Warmth and boundaries. Expectations and support. The limit and the relationship.

They need us to stay warm without becoming permissive. Firm without becoming frightening.

To hold the boundary with steady hands, and relationship with gentle ones. To lead without controlling, connect without surrendering, and to stay steady when feelings are running big.

Boundaries aren’t just about behaviour.

The boundaries we set with our children aren’t just about managing behaviour in the moment. They teach something much bigger. They teach children about what relationships can hold, whether love survives disagreement, and whether warmth and firmness can live in the same person at the same time.

So what actually is a boundary?

A boundary isn’t punishment, it isn’t a wall, and it isn’t a more polite way to control somebody else.

It isn’t something we demand from a child, and it isn’t something we do to them. It’s something we hold for them, and something we decide and hold in ourselves – for the safety and care of the child, the relationship, and everyone else involved.

It’s a statement of our behaviour, not a request for theirs. This is what I’ll do to keep us safe. This is how I’ll protect our relationship. These are the values I’ll keep leading us towards.

Our boundaries and decisions belong to us. We don’t need our children to agree with them.

Their feelings and responses belong to them. We don’t need to agree with those either – but we do need to make room for them. This is where warmth and strength meet.

You don’t have to like this. You don’t have to agree. You get to feel everything you feel. The boundary is still here – and so am I. I can take care of you while you move through the feelings that boundary brings.

Gentle parenting is still ‘it’. But it won’t always feel gentle.

The ‘gentle’ in gentle parenting isn’t softness, permissiveness, or keeping children happy. It isn’t the absence of boundaries. It’s the absence of fear, shame, and disconnection in the way we hold them. 

It means we don’t use fear, shame, humiliation, withdrawal of love or our greater size and authority to force compliance. We protect their dignity and the relationship – even while holding a boundary they fiercely dislike.

Gentle doesn’t mean: ‘You can have whatever you want because I don’t want you to be upset.‘

It means, ‘The answer is still no. You get to feel everything you feel about that – and I will take care of you through all of it.’

The boundary might be firm. The decision might be non-negotiable. The child might be spectacularly unimpressed. The ‘gentle’ is in refusing to make them afraid of us in order to make them listen to us.

When we set a boundary, we’re not withdrawing love or connection. We’re actually expressing it. We’re saying: I can see what you need right now, and I can also see what you need in the long run, and I love you enough to hold both of those things even when one of them is tricky for you or both of us.

When boundaries repeatedly arrive without warmth, a child may learn that love goes cold when there is disagreement, or struggle. That to be corrected is to be disconnected from. That the safest version of the person in front of them disappears a little when things get hard. That there’s no space for dissent without exile.

A boundary without firmness can teach something equally damaging – a child may learn that the people who care about them will eventually give in, that increasing their discomfort is a strategy that works, that relationships bend under enough pressure. This isn’t because the child is calculating or manipulative, but because children are exquisitely attuned to what works. They learn the system they’re given.

Neither is what we want to teach. Yet both can happen when we’re tired, when we’re overwhelmed, when we’re running on the parenting patterns we absorbed before we were old enough to question them.

The boundaries many of us inherited.

Many of us grew up with boundaries most of us grew up with that looked like one of two things: a wall, or nothing at all.

Either someone held the line so hard there was no warmth left on the other side of it, or they gave and gave until there was no line left to speak of. Many of us learned about boundaries from one of those two places – and most of us are still untangling it.

So when we hold boundaries with our own children, we bring all of that history with us: the fear of being too hard or too soft, the guilt of saying no, the exhaustion of holding something that keeps getting pushed. We bring the version of boundaries we were given in our own childhoods, even when we are trying to build something different.

In an instant, we can time-travel back to a world where boundaries simply happened, or didn’t.

For some of us, boundaries arrived as walls. We didn’t question them. We didn’t expect our feelings to change anything. There was no room for protest, disagreement or disappointment. We did as we were told – or learned what it would cost us not to. 

For others, the boundaries weren’t there at all. We might have learned to ‘untether’, and grew up making decisions and taking charge before we were ready. We might have become bigger, louder, or more demanding, hoping that someone, somewhere, might finally offer an edge that was steady enough to lean against, and safe enough to rely on.

Now, when our children push back, something old can rise in us. Perhaps we hear disrespect, and feel the shame that once followed it. Perhaps we feel invisible or erased, and remember the loneliness and unmattering that once came with it. Maybe we fear becoming too controlling. Or losing control. Perhaps we remember having no voice, or carrying decisions and responsibilities that should never have belonged to a child. 

And there we are, trying to parent the child in front of us while quietly negotiating with the child we used to be.

The two ways we lose the line.

When our child is distressed, their nervous system pulls on ours. Their distress can raise distress in us. We are wired to respond to it. When every instinct tells us to protect them from distress, and when holding the limit is the thing causing that distress, the urge to let it go, give in, or make it stop can be enormous. 

So some of us bend. We say yes when we meant no. We walk back the consequence. We absorb the storm to restore the calm. Afterwards, we often feel the unease of it – that quiet sense that something was lost in the giving, that the line moved again, that we’re not quite sure who’s leading anymore.

Others of us hold – but we hold in a way that closes something down. Our voices gets sharp. The warmth drains out. We stay firm but we go somewhere the child can’t reach us, and they feel that distance as much as they would have felt the consequence. They might comply, but repeated experiences like this can teach them that the relationship is conditional: I’ll care for you when you do what I want. In those moments, we risk becoming someone to avoid when things get messy, rather than someone to turn to. 

What we’re looking for is something harder than either of those. Something that requires us to stay regulated when everything in the moment is pulling us toward reactivity or retreat.

Why do children push against our boundaries?

When children have an important need, they’ll usually try the ‘right’ way to get it met first. If that doesn’t work, they’ll try the ‘big’ way. Louder. Messier. More determined. Sometimes with a soundtrack that could crack thunder. 

The need beneath the behaviour might be for autonomy, independence, power, influence. It might be a need to feel heard, seen or attended to, or not to miss out on something that feels so important right now.

All needs are valid. This doesn’t mean they can – or should – always be met. Sometimes other people have competing needs. Sometimes resources are stretched. Sometimes the answer simply has to be ‘no’.

And then comes the push.

Children might push against a boundary because they want it moved. Of course they do. They are excellent advocates for their immediate interests. (Aren’t we all at times?)

Sometimes though, they are also discovering whether the boundary, and the relationship, can hold their disappointment, their anger, their anxiety, their upset.

Imagine it like holding them the way a riverbank holds a river. Riverbanks don’t fight the river, and they don’t shame it for moving too fast or abandon it when it gets muddy. They give the river – all that energy – shape and direction. Without those banks, the water doesn’t become freer – it spreads, loses direction, and floods everything around it.

Our boundaries can work in the same way. They aren’t there to squash a child’s power. They’re there to contain it and guide it. Children can protest, disagree, and rage against the limit – and discover that neither the boundary nor our love has been washed away.

We don’t need to control them, and we don’t want to be controlled by them. We want them to feel that we can hold them, even when things get fast, messy, muddy.

When the edge disappears because we retreat, give in when we really don’t want to, or negotiate the limit into oblivion, a child may become calm in the moment, but repeated experiences can teach them that escalation moves the line, leaving our guidance and boundaries feeling uncertain. This isn’t because they are calculating or manipulative, but because children learn from what happens next. They learn what we teach them. If the boundary repeatedly moves under pressure, that’s what they most likely will learn.

When the boundary hardens into anger or humiliation, the edge is still there – but it stops feeling safe enough to lean against.

What we are reaching for is a riverbank that stays strong, without becoming stone-cold.

The two parts of a healthy boundary.

A healthy boundary needs warmth and strength.

Children need adults who can stay warm without becoming permissive. Firm without becoming frightening.

Adults who can lead without controlling, connect without surrendering, and stay steady when feelings are running big.

Warmth + boundaries. Expectations + support.

We weave warmth and expectations together, like threads.

Warmth says, ‘I’m with you. I see you. Your feelings matter. Our connection is safe.’

Strength says, ‘I know this is what I need to do now. You can depend on me. I’ve got you. I’ve got us. I’ve got this.’

One without the other leaves gaps. Woven together, they create something strong enough to hold both the child and the boundary.

The challenge is showing up with warmth and strength beautifully woven together on the outside – even when on the inside it feels more like a feral knot of helplessness and rage.

This is allowed. Calm leadership is not the absence of big feelings in us. It’s choosing who gets the microphone.

The most effective response.

The response most likely to help with tricky behaviour isn’t escalation and it isn’t capitulation.

Escalation abandons the connection: ‘I’ll become bigger, louder, or more frightening until you do what I say.’

Capitulation abandons the boundary, and often ourselves: ‘I’ll ignore what I know needs to happen, so your distress will stop.’

The response that’s most likely to help sits between them. It’s presence. Calm, persistent, presence that doesn’t depend on immediate compliance, or obedience from someone (a child) who can’t think clearly, rationally, or thoughtfully right now. None of us are able to think at our best when big feelings hijack the moment.

Of course, it’s remarkably easy for us to be recruited into their storm. The pull to make them stop, comply, obey, agree with us, can be seismic. But our authority isn’t about any of that. It doesn’t come from winning the moment or disappearing their big behaviour. It comes from outlasting it. Staying present through the toughness of the moment, without handing over to it.

It comes from being the person who doesn’t need to win the argument to prove that we’re in charge, or disappear from the argument to get peace. The one who is clear about what they will and won’t accept,  then acts from that place – not with force, threat, or fear, but with quiet, steady follow-through. 

Children need adults who are anchored. Adults who can be moved by their feelings without being swept away by them. Adults who lead from warmth rather than fear, hold from care rather than control, and protect both the boundary and the relationship.

This might sound something like ,‘I know you don’t like this. I know it feels unfair. The boundary is still here. And so am I. I’m not changing my mind.’

That kind of presence isn’t about control. It’s about being a safe enough, warm enough, confident enough (even if we need to fake it sometimes) presence that the child doesn’t have to be in charge of what happens next. 

It’s not about control. It’s about recognising what belongs to whom and honouring that.

Our boundaries and decisions are ours. We don’t need our children to agree. 

Their feelings and responses belong to them. We don’t need to agree with those either – but we do need to keep the relationship, and the bodies (theirs, ours, others) safe while those feelings move through.

Warm and immovable.

The goal isn’t a boundary that’s hard. It’s a boundary that holds and stays warm while it does.

This looks like a parent who can say no and mean it, without the no becoming cold. A parent who can hold a consequence without withdrawing. A parent who can stay present in the discomfort of their child’s disappointment, sadness, frustration, without either rescuing them from it or hardening against it.

We don’t need to be perfect. Not even a little bit.

Be warm and strong. At the same time. It isn’t simple.

Warm boundaries require something underneath them. Rest. Support. Repair. Enough genuine restoration that we are not trying to summon our wisest selves while running on fumes and cold toast.

This matters more than we often realise.

A regulated parent isn’t a luxury. It’s the foundation everything else is built on. Regulation creates more room for warmth, flexibility, and thoughtful follow-through.

Depletion makes warm boundaries much harder to hold. It narrows the space between a cold boundary and giving in. 

Of course, there will be weeks, seasons, or years, when keeping our tanks full enough will have the ease of splitting an atom with a chainsaw. Some days we can be exactly the parents we want to be. And some days we can’t. 

Those ‘can’t’ days, can grow our children in other ways. This is where we model imperfection, humility, grace, empathy – for them and for ourselves.

‘I’m really sorry for [yelling]. That probably felt  [confusing] for you. Next time [I’ll try to slow things down or take a minute]. Can we try that again?’

What warm boundaries actually sound like.

‘Warm and firm’ is easy to understand from a peaceful distance. It’s much harder to do in the moment, especially when the moment is loud.

Here’s what it can sound like.

When screen time ends:

Screen time is done, but your child isn’t. Rather than getting louder:

‘I said no! End of story. Why do you do this every time?’

Or ignoring them or negotiating until the original boundary quietly evaporates:

‘Okay, five more minutes. No, really – five.’ Then, ‘You’ve had an extra 30 minutes. This is absolutely the last five. Ok?’

Try: ‘I know you’re not done, and I get that’s really frustrating. We’re still turning it off now. I’ll sit with you for a bit while you settle.’

Or: ‘I expected that you wouldn’t like this. You’re allowed to not like it. I’m not changing my mind.’

The limit is clear. The tone stays warm. You’re not arguing, over-explaining, threatening, or negotiating.

Not louder, not harder, just steadily there. Leadership through care, not control.

Your child says something hurtful.

Rather than: ‘Don’t speak to me like that! Go to your room right now!’

The limit might hold, or the child might get louder. Either way, the connection can contract. If this becomes a pattern, the child may learn that the relationship becomes less safe when their feelings are difficult. 

Or this: ‘I know you didn’t mean it. It’s okay.’

Connection holds, but the behaviour disappears into it. The child learns that the way they speak to people they love doesn’t really matter.

Try this: ‘I can hear how angry you are. I’m not going to listen when you’re speaking to me like that. I’m right here. When you’re ready we can try again. Take your time. You’re not in trouble.’

The parent doesn’t match the escalation, retreat from it, or pretend it didn’t happen.

They hold their position, and they hold the relationship.

That’s what a boundary is – the steps we take to protect physical safety, relational safety, and the values we want them to move forward into the world with (such as kindness, respect, honesty, courage).

A child can only receive our guidance or our correction when they feel safe enough to turn towards us. This is why our presence matters – warm and strong. 

The script isn’t magic. They might still escalate. The boundary might still be hard to hold. Safety, developmental capacity, neurodivergence, and the demands of the moment will always matter more than perfect words. But when our warmth doesn’t drain away as your firmness rises, children can learn something deeper than compliance. They learn:

My important adult can handle this.
The boundary is real.
The relationship is safe.
I don’t have to be in charge here.

That is what children rest in. And when children can truly rest in the care of their adults, they don’t need to spend as much energy scanning for safety, testing where the edges are, or trying to stay in control. That frees them for the work of childhood – to learn, to play, to think, to imagine, to connect, and to become.

What they’re learning.

Every boundary teaches your child something about relationships.

They’re learning whether love stays under pressure, such as when needs clash. Whether people can disagree and stay connected. Whether their voice matters even when it doesn’t change the answer. Whether it’s safe to disagree, to share their thoughts, their opinions, or whether only one person can ever be right which means someone will always be wrong. Whether their distress has the power to move the people around them in ways that aren’t good for anyone. Whether strength and kindness can live in the same person at the same time.

We don’t have to be perfect for this. We need to be warm enough that children feel safe with us, and steady enough that they feel safe against us – enough of the time.

The softest people can hold the firmest lines. The warmest voice can say the clearest no.

The goal isn’t to become a parent who never raises their voice, never gets it wrong, or never needs to repair. It’s to become someone whose warmth doesn’t disappear when the boundary arrives. Someone whose strength doesn’t require fear. Someone whose love doesn’t retreat when things become difficult.

Children don’t rest in our perfection. They rest in the growing certainty that we can hold them and the limits that keep them safe, at the same time.
